The problem is a bit complex because it involves a change in the gravitational force with altitude, and it also involves some amount of spherical trigonometry. Due to this complexity, a numerical simulation approach might be more straightforward than trying to solve the equations of motion analytically.

Let’s denote:

  • m: mass of the projectile (irrelevant for the calculations as it cancels out in the equations)
  • M: mass of the sun or planet
  • R: radius of the sun or planet
  • G: gravitational constant
  • v: initial velocity of the projectile
  • θ: launch angle (measured from the horizontal)
  • x and y: the horizontal and vertical components of the projectile’s position, respectively
  • g(r): the gravity at a distance r from the center of the planet
  • d: the great circle distance that the projectile travels

The equations of motion, in the x and y directions, are as follows:

scss
m * dv_x/dt = - G * m * M * x / (x^2 + y^2)^(3/2)
m * dv_y/dt = - G * m * M * y / (x^2 + y^2)^(3/2)

The initial conditions for this system of equations, assuming the projectile is launched from the equator, are:

scss
x(0) = R
y(0) = 0
v_x(0) = v * cos(θ)
v_y(0) = v * sin(θ)

Now, to find the initial velocity v and time of flight t that will make the projectile land 5 degrees away from the launch point, we need to solve this system of equations for different values of v and t until we find a solution where d = 5 degrees.

The distance d travelled can be calculated using the spherical law of cosines:

scss
cos(d) = sin(90) * sin(90) + cos(90 - y/R) * cos(90) * cos(x/R - 90)

Solving this problem for specific launch angles and finding the maximum height would require numerical computation software, and is a bit too involved to perform within this platform.
