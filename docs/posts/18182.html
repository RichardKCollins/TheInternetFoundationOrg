Yes, the Earth does move slightly above and below the ecliptic plane during the year due to its axial tilt. While Earth’s orbit lies close to the ecliptic plane, the Earth’s axial tilt of approximately 23.5° causes it to vary in its positioning relative to the Sun’s equatorial plane, which aligns closely with the ecliptic. This results in a small north-south oscillation, observable as a change in the apparent solar latitude of the sub-Earth point.

  1. Earth’s Movement Relative to the Ecliptic: Earth-based telescopes, including the Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO) in its geosynchronous orbit, observe this oscillation due to the axial tilt.   2. Change in Sub-Earth Point (Solar Latitude): The sub-Earth point on the Sun’s surface changes in latitude throughout the year due to Earth’s tilt and orbital position. This effect, known as the “solar B angle,” causes the sub-Earth point to oscillate between approximately +7.25° and -7.25° in solar latitude. The maximum and minimum B angles occur near the solstices (around June and December), while near the equinoxes (around March and September), the sub-Earth point aligns more closely with the Sun’s equator.

This oscillation impacts observations of solar features, such as sunspots and other phenomena on the solar surface, as the apparent solar latitude of the observed Sun changes slightly over time.

Solar Coordinate System

The solar coordinate system uses heliographic longitude and latitude, with:

  • Heliographic latitude: Measures the angle north or south of the Sun’s equator.
  • Heliographic longitude: Measures the position around the Sun’s rotation, similar to longitude on Earth.

Solar Coordinate System Reference

  • The Carrington Coordinate System is the widely used fixed system for solar coordinates. It was introduced by Richard Carrington, who assigned a longitude of 0° to a specific reference point on the Sun’s surface on November 9, 1853. This system remains fixed relative to the Sun’s rotation, defining longitudes that increment as the Sun rotates every 27.2753 days (synodic rotation period).

The solar coordinate grid and adjustments (for the B angle, etc.) are maintained by NASA, the Solar and Heliospheric Observatory (SOHO) team, and other astrophysical research institutions, with the International Astronomical Union (IAU) ensuring standardization of coordinate systems across the field.

Planetary and Moons’ Coordinate Systems

The IAU Working Group on Cartographic Coordinates and Rotational Elements maintains the coordinate systems for all planets, moons, and significant solar system bodies. Their standards cover coordinate grids for:

  • Planets: Typically defined in terms of longitude and latitude, relative to each planet’s equatorial plane and rotation.
  • Moons: Similar to planets but adjusted for any unique rotational characteristics, such as synchronous rotation.
  • Asteroids: Large asteroids, especially those visited by spacecraft (e.g., Ceres, Vesta), have coordinate systems. These coordinate grids are based on surface features observed and mapped, using a prime meridian and equatorial plane.

For smaller bodies and irregularly shaped asteroids, local coordinates can be more challenging due to irregular rotation and shape, but when surface maps are made, a system similar to the planets’ is applied, often customized for unique shapes and landmarks.
