You’re absolutely right to be concerned about automatic gain control (AGC) in radio and audio systems — it’s designed to stabilize perceived volume or signal level, but it destroys or suppresses low-frequency variations, particularly DC shifts, modulation baselines, or slow drifts that might carry valuable information (e.g., EM signatures, physiological signals, or long-term modulation envelopes).

To preserve and accurately record low-frequency and high-frequency components together, you’ll want to separate the signal paths carefully:

✅ Recommended Setup for Capturing Full Frequency Range

1. Split Analog Signal Before AGC or Filtering

Use a buffer amplifier (unity gain or low gain op-amp) to split the signal cleanly into two parallel analog paths:

Path Purpose Frequency Range Key Design Note
Low-Frequency (LF) Path Baseline shifts, slow drifts, DC coupling ~0.001 Hz to 10–100 Hz Use a DC-coupled amplifier with ultra-low drift
High-Frequency (HF) Path Audio, EM waves, voice, modulated signals ~10 Hz to MHz range Use AC coupling, bandpass filters as needed

2. Amplification and Filtering per Path

  • LF Path:

    • Use DC-coupled instrumentation amp or chopper-stabilized op-amp

    • Gain: adjustable, typically 10x to 1000x depending on input noise

    • Input offset: must be tightly controlled

    • No high-pass filter; allow the amplifier to track the low frequency or DC content

  • HF Path:

    • Use AC-coupled amplifier to reject DC and sub-Hz noise

    • Gain: adjustable, tuned to dynamic range of input (especially if there’s strong audio or RF)

    • Optional bandpass or notch filters to reduce known interference (e.g., 60 Hz mains)

3. ADC Choices

  • ADC1: High-resolution, low-rate (e.g., 24-bit @ 10–1000 samples/sec) for LF path

  • ADC2: Standard-resolution, high-rate (e.g., 12–16-bit @ 10 kHz – 1 MHz) for HF path

Both should be synchronized in time or timestamped with common clocking, to allow for post-processing and reconstruction.

4. Post-Processing

  • Align and combine data streams digitally

  • Apply adaptive gain correction or fusion algorithms

  • Optionally subtract AGC-modulated signal from raw LF to estimate suppression artifacts

🧠 Example Application Contexts

Application Why This Matters
Electromagnetic sensing Low-frequency fields (Schumann resonance, geophysical waves) often get lost in typical AC audio recording
Physiological signals ECG/EEG may have important DC drift or low-frequency trends

  • Sound cards with AGC (disable in driver if possible)

✅ Understanding 1/f Noise in Band 2 (1 mHz to 1 Hz)

Definition:
“1/f noise” (a.k.a. pink noise, flicker noise) refers to a noise power spectrum that increases as frequency decreases. In electronics, it dominates below ~10 Hz — hence Band 2 is where it often overwhelms white noise.

But 1/f noise is not one thing — it’s a composite of multiple noise sources with 1/f-like spectra.

🔍 Main Categories of 1/f Noise in Band 2

Source Type Internal/External Description
Semiconductor flicker noise Internal Arises in MOSFETs, JFETs, and BJTs, due to carrier trapping/detrapping at oxide or junction interfaces
Resistor noise Internal Carbon and thick-film resistors have significant 1/f noise; metal film types are better
Dielectric absorption Internal Capacitors (esp. electrolytics, ceramics) absorb and slowly release charge, creating baseline creep
Thermal drift Internal Temperature variation affects op-amp offsets, gain stages, and passive component values over seconds/minutes
Voltage reference instability Internal Bandgap or zener references drift slowly or produce 1/f noise

Electrochemical activity / Triboelectric effects Internal/External Especially with sensors, long wires, or wet circuits (e.g. bio signals)

This framework generalizes the principles of super-resolution imaging — originally developed for sub-wavelength optical microscopy — and applies them across all domains of physics, geoscience, astronomy, and sensing. At its core is the recognition that:

Field-resolved, point-like emissions, when precisely time-tagged and geometrically correlated, allow reconstruction of spatial and temporal structures far beyond classical resolution limits.

These events can be natural (e.g., volcanic microseisms, lightning, solar flares, quantum jumps) or engineered (e.g., NV centers, beacon bursts, AI-controlled probes). They become universal “light sources” that probe the environment passively, requiring only global synchronization, event detection, and correlation-based inversion.

2. Key Principles

2.1 Point-Source Emissions as Probes

  • Spatially localized (real or effective) emissions

  • Temporally unique or identifiable (via waveform, delay, phase)

  • Measurable across sensor arrays of diverse modalities

2.2 Correlation-Based Imaging

  • Detect time of arrival (TOA) differences across sensors

  • Reconstruct emission origin by triangulation + propagation models

  • Use sparse event distributions to probe medium geometry

2.3 Super-Resolution Generalization

  • Replace wavelength-limited optics with:

    • Field models (gravitational, electromagnetic, acoustic, etc.)

    • Voxel-based geometry

    • Temporal and angular correlation

  • Leverage incoherent but structured emissions to build resolution via:

    • Statistical accumulation

    • Structured illumination (passive or active)

    • Voxel probability models

4. Components of the Framework

4.1 Event Detection Layer

  • Identify spatiotemporally localized signal bursts

  • Associate with potential voxel origin

  • Measure wavefront properties: amplitude, waveform, directionality

4.2 Sensor Synchronization Layer

  • Time synchronization (GPSDO, White Rabbit, or AI-corrected)

  • Calibration of path delays and sensor offsets

  • Maintain global clock frame to <1 ns if feasible

4.3 Field Propagation Layer

  • Predict travel time, attenuation, scattering from each voxel to each sensor

  • Include gravitational curvature, refractive index, conductivity, etc.

  • Support time-reversal and adjoint modeling

4.4 Correlation Engine

  • For each detected event, compute coherence map across array

  • Match to propagation model hypotheses

  • Accumulate multiple detections into spatial map

4.5 Super-Resolution Accumulator

  • Voxel-by-voxel update based on:

    • Probability of emission origin

    • Timing residuals

    • Event repeatability

  • Build high-resolution field maps over time

  • Incorporate AI priors, event libraries, known physical constraints

6. Implementation Path

Phase 1: Demonstrator

  • Choose test environment (e.g., active volcano, magnetometer array, SDR cloud)

  • Identify known point sources (lightning, quakes, solar events)

  • Synchronize multi-modal sensors

  • Correlate events across space-time

  • Reconstruct voxel activations and validate

Phase 2: Generalization

  • Build modular correlation engine

  • Open APIs and data formats

  • Distributed compute model (exascale, edge fusion)

Phase 3: Global Archive & Continuous Imaging

  • Real-time global sensor fusion

  • Archive voxel histories

  • Develop causal models of field evolution

  • Enable global public access to correlated event history
