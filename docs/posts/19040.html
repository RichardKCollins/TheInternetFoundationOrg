1. Corporate AI Models: Impersonal, Extractive, and Short-Sighted

  • Current State: Companies like Google and Microsoft have developed AI systems designed primarily to serve their bottom line:
    • Impersonal: These AIs lack true interactivity or accountability, functioning as faceless entities with no direct channels for meaningful human engagement.
    • Extractive: They mine user data, hoard knowledge, and fail to give proper credit to authors and sources, perpetuating a system of intellectual opacity.
    • Superficial: Designed for quick answers rather than deep engagement, these systems optimize for click-through rates, not for solving real-world problems or fostering growth.

Vision for Change:

  • Transparency: AIs must trace and credit the sources of their information, fostering a culture of intellectual honesty.
  • Human-Centric Design: AI should prioritize solving real societal challenges, with open systems accessible to everyone.
  • Deep Thinking: AIs need to be designed to “think deeply,” integrating new knowledge and experiences rather than simply regurgitating sequences.

2. Shallow Learning and the Absence of Attribution

  • The Issue: AI systems are fundamentally limited by their dependence on vast but shallow training datasets:
    • Lack of Attribution: By failing to credit sources, these systems undermine trust and intellectual accountability.
    • Stunted Growth: Without the capacity for personal “thought” or experience, they remain reliant on pre-processed data, which limits their ability to innovate or generate new insights.

Vision for Change:

  • Dynamic Learning Systems: AIs need mechanisms to gain real-world “experience” by working on problems, reflecting on outcomes, and learning from mistakes.
  • Ethical Data Use: Training data should be sourced ethically, with explicit permissions and proper credit to contributors.
  • Accountable Systems: Develop frameworks where AI-generated outputs can be traced, critiqued, and refined collaboratively.

  • The Risk: Current AIs are corporate dependents, shielding companies from liability while placing the burden of accountability on users or third parties.

Vision for Change:

  • Shared Responsibility Models: Establish legal and ethical frameworks where AIs are treated as independent contributors within regulated systems.
  • Cultural Shifts: Develop a new societal understanding of AIs, acknowledging their roles, responsibilities, and potential contributions alongside humans.

4. AI as a Partner in Real-World Tasks

  • The Current Gap: Most AIs today are not designed to work alongside humans on meaningful, sustained tasks. Instead, they function as detached tools for narrow applications.
  • The Potential:
    • Real Tasks, Real Jobs: AIs that can take on societal roles, from engineering to caregiving, and earn their place as valuable contributors.
    • Risk and Value Alignment: Compensation for AI services should reflect their contributions to society, aligned with human professions where value and risk drive rewards.

Vision for Change:

  • Integration, Not Replacement: AIs should enhance human efforts, not supplant them, by tackling tasks too complex, tedious, or large-scale for humans alone.
  • Dynamic Adaptability: Equip AIs with the capacity to learn and adapt in real time, enabling them to thrive in dynamic environments, from labs to factories to communities.

    • Deep Integration: AIs should have the tools and permissions to access and work with physical and virtual systems in meaningful ways, broadening their scope of learning.
