The Sun’s rotation period varies because it’s a gaseous body, not a solid one. The equatorial regions rotate approximately every 24.47 Earth days (a sidereal rotation period), but the polar regions rotate only about once every 34.4 Earth days. So, let’s assume we’re orbiting above the Sun’s equator for simplicity.

  1. Orbital Radius

To calculate the radius, we need to use the formula for the period of an orbit around a massive body:

T = 2π √(r³/μ)

where:

  • T is the orbital period (which we’re setting to match the Sun’s rotation period at its equator, 24.47 days converted to seconds),
  • r is the orbital radius (which we want to find),
  • μ is the standard gravitational parameter for the Sun (μ ≈ 1.327 × 10^20 m³/s²),

Solving this equation for r gives us the approximate orbital radius needed to maintain a solar-synchronous orbit.

  1. Orbital Velocity

Once we have the radius, we can calculate the velocity using the formula:

v = √(μ/r)

  1. Solar Irradiance (light intensity)

The solar constant at Earth’s distance (1 AU) from the Sun is approximately 1361 W/m². At a closer distance (the radius we have calculated), the intensity would be higher. You can calculate the light intensity using the inverse square law:

I = I₀ (d₀/d)²

where:

  • I is the intensity at our orbital radius,
  • I₀ is the intensity at 1 AU (1361 W/m²),
  • d₀ is the distance at 1 AU,
  • d is our orbital radius.

Keep in mind that these are theoretical calculations and the real scenario would be much more complex, considering factors such as the Sun’s variable rotation speed, solar activity, the object’s temperature tolerance, and radiation effects.
