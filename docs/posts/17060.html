When two conductors are charged, there indeed will be a force between them due to the electric fields created by the charges on each conductor. Here’s how this works, explained in steps: ### Basic Principle: – **Coulomb’s Law**: The force between two point charges \( q_1 \) and \( q_2 \) separated by a distance \( r \) in a vacuum is given by: \[ F = k_e \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2} \] where \( k_e \) is Coulomb’s constant (\(8.9875 \times 10^9 \, \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2 \)). ### Application to Conductors: – **Charged Conductors**: When a conductor is connected to a voltage source, it charges until it reaches a potential equal to the source voltage. The charge \( Q \) on a conductor can be related to the voltage \( V \) by: \[ Q = C \cdot V \] where \( C \) is the capacitance of the conductor. ### Force Between Charged Conductors: – **Parallel Plate Capacitor**: Consider a simple parallel plate capacitor where each plate has an area \( A \) and they are separated by a distance \( d \). If a voltage \( V \) is applied, each plate will have an equal and opposite charge \( Q \). The force between the plates can be derived from the energy stored in the capacitor or directly from the electric field. – **Electric Field**: The electric field \( E \) between the plates is: \[ E = \frac{V}{d} \] – **Force**: The force \( F \) on each plate due to the electric field can be approximated by considering the charge on one plate and the field due to the other: \[ F = Q \cdot E = Q \cdot \frac{V}{d} \] Substituting \( Q = CV \): \[ F = C \cdot V \cdot \frac{V}{d} = \frac{C V^2}{d} \] ### Examples: 1. **Parallel Plate Capacitor**: – If you have a capacitor with capacitance \( C = 1 \, \mu\text{F} \), voltage \( V = 100 \, \text{V} \), and separation \( d = 1 \, \text{mm} \): \[ F = \frac{1 \times 10^{-6} \times (100)^2}{1 \times 10^{-3}} = 10 \, \text{N} \] 2. **Spherical Conductors**: – For two spherical conductors, the force calculation becomes more complex due to the geometry, but the principle remains. If you have two spheres with charges \( Q_1 \) and \( Q_2 \) at a distance \( r \), you can use Coulomb’s law directly if the distance is large compared to their radii. ### Practical Considerations: – **Real Systems**: In real systems, the force might not be as straightforward due to edge effects, the shape of conductors, and the presence of dielectrics. However, the basic principle of electric force due to charges holds. – **Energy Method**: Alternatively, you can calculate the force by considering the change in energy of the system when the separation changes slightly. This method involves differentiating the energy stored in the capacitor with respect to distance. ### Summary: – **Force exists** between charged conductors due to the electric fields they generate. – **Capacitance and voltage** determine the charge, which in turn determines the force through electric field interactions. – **Calculations** can be simplified for idealized geometries like parallel plates but require more complex analysis for other shapes. This explanation should give you a good foundation on how and why forces exist between charged conductors, especially in the context of capacitors.

In MEMS (Microelectromechanical Systems) sensors, both resonant circuits and charge-to-digital converters are indeed used for high-precision measurements. Here’s an overview of each: ### Resonant Circuits in MEMS: **Principle**: – **Resonance**: A MEMS resonator can be designed to oscillate at a specific frequency, which is highly sensitive to changes in capacitance due to mechanical movement or environmental changes. – **Frequency Shift**: Changes in the capacitance (due to movement, pressure, acceleration, etc.) shift the resonant frequency of the circuit. This frequency shift can be measured with high precision. **Applications**: – **Accelerometers**: Detect acceleration by the shift in frequency when the proof mass moves, changing the capacitance. – **Gyroscopes**: Use the Coriolis effect to change the resonant frequency based on rotation. – **Pressure Sensors**: Changes in pressure cause a diaphragm to move, altering the capacitance and thus the resonant frequency. **Precision**: – **Frequency Measurement**: Modern electronics can measure frequency changes with resolutions down to parts per billion (ppb) or even better. This translates to detecting extremely small movements or changes in capacitance. – **Typical Resolution**: For MEMS devices, frequency changes can often be resolved to the sub-Hz level in a kHz or MHz range, allowing for detection of nanometer-scale movements or pressure changes. ### Charge-to-Digital Converters (CDC): **Principle**: – **Charge Sensing**: These converters measure the charge on a capacitor directly, which can be related to the physical parameter being sensed (like displacement, pressure, etc.). – **Conversion**: The charge is converted into a digital signal, which can then be processed for analysis. **Applications**: – **Capacitive Sensors**: Used in touchscreens, humidity sensors, or any application where a change in capacitance needs to be detected. – **Electrometer Applications**: For measuring extremely small currents or charges, often in scientific instruments or medical devices. **Precision**: – **Resolution**: Modern CDCs can achieve resolutions in the attofarad (aF) range or even better for capacitance changes. This corresponds to detecting charge changes in the range of femtocoulombs (fC) or less. – **Noise Floor**: The noise floor of these devices can be incredibly low, allowing for the detection of very small changes in charge or capacitance. For instance, some high-end CDCs might have noise floors in the range of 1 aF/√Hz. ### Comparative Advantages: – **Resonant Circuits**: – **Advantage**: High sensitivity to small changes, often with better inherent noise rejection due to the frequency domain operation. – **Disadvantage**: Can be more complex in design and might require more power due to the need for sustaining oscillations. – **Charge-to-Digital Converters**: – **Advantage**: Direct measurement of charge or capacitance changes without the need for an oscillating circuit, potentially simpler in some applications. – **Disadvantage**: Might be more susceptible to noise or require careful shielding and grounding to maintain precision. ### Current Technology: – **MEMS Resonators**: Advancements have led to MEMS resonators with quality factors (Q) in the thousands or even tens of thousands, allowing for very high precision in frequency measurements. – **Charge-to-Digital Converters**: Modern CDCs are integrated into many sensor systems, with some high-end models offering resolutions down to the zeptocoulomb (zC) range for charge detection, which is incredibly precise. ### Conclusion: Both technologies have evolved to provide extremely high precision in MEMS sensors. The choice between using resonant circuits or CDCs often depends on the specific application requirements, such as power consumption, environmental robustness, and the nature of the physical parameter being measured. Advances in both areas continue to push the boundaries of what’s measurable at the micro and nano scales.
